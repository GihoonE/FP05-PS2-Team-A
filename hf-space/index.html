    About & evidence

    What this Space shows, and what it cannot.

    Decision setting

    Six project teams share 100 university GPU-hours. Each team has an observable GPU request (up to 30 hours), estimated emissions of 0.24 kg CO₂e per GPU-hour, and a private project value from 1 to 10. Requests add up to more than the capacity, so a rule must decide who runs.

    Rational benchmark

    • First come, first served: the outcome depends only on the random arrival order and request sizes. Project value plays no role, so there is nothing to misreport.
    • Scored group selection (VCG): reporting your true value is a dominant strategy (DSIC). It is optimal whatever the others report, when utility = true value − own carbon penalty − payment and priority credits have a real future cost. The What if slider lets you check this in every game.
    • A player who understands the rule can predict their own selection from the scores and the 100-hour limit.

    Behavioral alternatives we look for

    • Overreporting to raise the chance of being selected.
    • Underreporting to save credits, carried over from pay-your-bid auction intuition.
    • Ranking heuristic: predicting winners by going down the score ranking instead of comparing whole groups.
    • Single-winner model: expecting only one team to win, as in a classic second-price auction.
    • Queue fairness: preferring first come, first served as fairer even when it wastes capacity or adds emissions.

    Evidence labels

    • observed your own choices in this session: request, report, prediction, reflection. This page does not store them; you choose whether to submit them through the class form.
    • simulated the five rival teams. They report truthfully and are drawn from the repo's Low / Medium / High tiers (15 h·3, 20 h·6, 25 h·9).
    • computed allocations, payments, utilities and the What if curve. They come from a port of the repo code, checked against the repo outputs.
    • planned classroom peer-play. Players submit their copied result lines through a Google Form, and we compare predictions, reports and preferences with the computed outcomes.

    Limits of classroom evidence

    • Small and self-selected: classmates at one symposium, not a random sample of GPU users.
    • Hypothetical stakes: priority credits here have no real future cost, which weakens the DSIC condition.
    • Simulated, truthful rivals: real teams may misreport or coordinate.
    • The interface itself frames choices (default report = true value, the order of explanations).
    • So the results are exploratory. They can challenge an assumption, but they are not population-level causal evidence.
