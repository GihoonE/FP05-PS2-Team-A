\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
University GPU clusters mostly allocate by queue. Replaying 20.9 million academic jobs, FAIR-Compute finds that value-blind rules recover about 65\% of available scientific value; it leaves carbon and truthful mechanisms to future work~\cite{fair_compute2026}. Truthful cloud auctions price a provider's server cost~\cite{zhang2015iaas}, and carbon-aware schedulers shift flexible load to clean hours without prices or reports~\cite{radovanovic2021carbon}. No rule we found combines a carbon objective with incentives for honest reporting.

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}
Self-declared value will be exaggerated, and carbon the rule ignores is paid by the public. An institution needs an efficient, carbon-aware, auditable rule whose incentives survive the way payments are made.

\section{Application Scenario}\label{sec:scenario}
A university cluster offers $K=100$ GPU-hours to six teams A--F requesting $d=(25,20,15,25,20,15)$ (120 hours in total). Each team privately knows its project value $v_i\in\{3,6,9\}$; requests and emissions $e_i=0.24d_i$ kg CO$_2$e are public, and the institution announces a carbon weight $\lambda=0.5$. All parameters are stated, synthetic assumptions.

\section{Research Question}\label{sec:rq}
Can a published, carbon-aware VCG rule allocate GPU-hours with more value net of carbon than first come, first served (FCFS), while making truthful reporting optimal---and under which payment conditions? It joins economics, computation, and behavior.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Game.} Players: the six teams. Strategy: one report $r_i$, submitted simultaneously (static game, incomplete information). Payoff: $u_i=v_i-\lambda e_i-p_i$ if selected, else 0.
\textbf{Rule.} Score $s_i=r_i-\lambda e_i$; select the feasible group $S^*$ maximizing $\sum_{S}s_i$ subject to $\sum_{S}d_i\le K$, by exact search over all 64 groups; each winner pays the Clarke--Groves externality $p_i=W^*_{-i}-\sum_{j\in S^*\setminus\{i\}}s_j$~\cite{clarke1971,groves1973} in priority credits (10 per score unit).
\textbf{Credit design.} \emph{C1:} each selected team pays its own carbon charge \ensuremath{\lambda e_i} in credits (else the best report is \ensuremath{v_i+\lambda e_i}). \emph{C2 (assumption):} a charged credit has a linear opportunity cost, as in $u_i$. \emph{C3 (check):} a fixed 100-credit budget per round, no top-ups, retired payments; it does not bind in the tested cases. Under C1 and C2 truthful reporting is dominant by the Groves argument (Appendix~A); C3 shows feasibility, not that credits have real value.
\textbf{Three lenses.} Game theory predicts truthful reports; social choice sets the objective, value net of carbon, with two trade-offs: efficiency against access and truthfulness against budget balance~\cite{greenlaffont1977}; mechanism design specifies and tests the rule.
\textbf{Retained PS2 comparison.} Random-order FCFS over all 720 arrival orders. Analogy boundary: only with one slot and $\lambda=0$ is the rule a Vickrey second-price auction~\cite{vickrey1961} (unit-tested); otherwise several teams win under a knapsack constraint and pay in credits, not money.
\textbf{Checks.} Report grid $\{0,0.5,\dots,10\}$ for every team at each $\lambda\in\{0,0.1,\dots,1\}$; two GPU pools; C3. \textbf{Behavior.} Space players report and predict before seeing the outcome.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\emph{Computed, one synthetic instance} (Appendix~A). VCG selects $\{A,B,D,E\}$ with carbon-adjusted score 19.2; FCFS scores 15.6--19.2 over 720 orders (mean 16.96), and no order beats VCG. Emissions per unit of value fall from 0.81--0.84 to 0.72 kg CO$_2$e, but VCG serves 4 teams against an FCFS mean of 4.93. Winners pay 24 credits (96 in total) and, with the carbon charge, spend 48--54 of 100; the largest tested spend is 60, so the budget does not bind. No tested report beats the truth at any tested $\lambda$; $\lambda$ moves prices, not the best strategy, so it is a policy weight (here, above 1.25 only two teams are served). Two GPU pools: same winners, 21.6 to 16.6 kg. \emph{Observed, exploratory} ($n=5$ author self-plays): no misreport paid, yet three of five overreported. 8 unit tests and 10 notebook checks pass.

\section{Intellectual Merits}\label{sec:merits}
Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} marks what is inherited (blue), new (orange), and evidence (green). The closest work, FAIR-Compute~\cite{fair_compute2026} measures the loss from value-blind rules at trace scale without incentives or carbon; we trade scale for an exact instance and build the rule it leaves open. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2015iaas} prove truthful cloud auctions with a server-cost term and money; we price carbon, pay in credits, state C1 and C2, and check feasibility (C3). Radovanovi\'c et al.~\cite{radovanovic2021carbon} shift load to clean hours without prices; we obtain a similar placement inside a strategic rule, with fixed carbon rates. Code checks showed that utility $v_i-p_i$ rewards overreporting (computation changed the economics); self-plays showed truth optimal but not typical (behavior qualified it). Bound: one round, one instance, assumed rates.

\section{Practical Impacts}\label{sec:impacts}
Figure~\ref{fig:impact} (Appendix~G) places the official SDG icons on the mechanism~\cite{un_sdg}. \textbf{Target 9.5} (research capability): carbon-adjusted value 16.96 to 19.2. \textbf{Target 9.4} (resource-use efficiency): 0.81--0.84 to 0.72 kg CO$_2$e per unit of value, 0.55 with two pools. Both are \emph{demonstrated only in simulation}. \textbf{Target 13.3} (institutional capacity on mitigation): a published $\lambda$, a fixed credit supply, and a re-runnable log; \emph{intended}, as no institution has adopted the rule. Stakeholders: teams, administrators, the institution, the public. Risks (safeguards): heavy $\lambda$ trades 9.5 for 9.4 (calibrate and publish $\lambda$ from local data; 1.25 is only this instance's threshold); repeated exclusion (history-based credits, planned); assumed credit value (repeated-round pilot); unverifiable values (audit log, ex-post pilot).
